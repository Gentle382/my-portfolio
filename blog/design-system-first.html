Building a Design System Before the Screens

On the Dominion City Kubwa app I set up colours, type, spacing and components before designing a single final screen. Here is why, and what it bought the project.

Chibueze Wisdom UgochukwuUI/UX & Product Designer

Dominion City Kubwa wanted one app for church life: this week’s events, communities, video messages, giving and an account. That is five separate journeys, used by two very different people. A regular member wants to get in and out quickly. A first-time visitor needs to be welcomed and guided.

With that many journeys, the risk is that each one ends up looking slightly different. A button here is 40px tall, a card there has a different corner radius, a grey label is a slightly different grey. None of it is dramatic on its own. Together it makes an app feel unfinished, and it makes the developers’ job harder.

Why screens-first breaks down

The usual way to start is to open Figma and design the home screen, because that is the most exciting one. Then the media screen, then giving. Every decision gets made in the moment, inside one screen, and then copied or re-made in the next.

By the fifth screen you have made the same decision five times, and probably not the same way each time. When a developer asks “which blue is the right blue?”, there is no single answer.

Every decision I make once in the system is a decision I don’t have to make again on every screen.

So after research and grayscale wireframes, and before any high-fidelity screens, I stopped and built a small system.

What went into the system

I kept it deliberately small. A church app does not need a hundred tokens. It needs a handful that are chosen well and used everywhere.

Colour

A confident royal blue drawn from the church’s identity, a deep navy for headings and pressed states, a light sky tint for surfaces, and an ink colour for body text. I checked every text and background pair against WCAG AA before any screen used it.

Type

One family, Poppins, in four sizes: a 28px semibold heading, a 20px semibold title, 16px body text and 13px captions. Four sizes is enough for a mobile app and few enough that nobody has to guess.

Spacing, shape and touch

An 8-point spacing scale, a 12px corner radius on cards and buttons, and a 44px minimum touch target for anything tappable. That last one matters for an app people open on a Sunday morning, often one-handed, sometimes in a hurry.

Components

The core set was small: a primary button (“Give now”), a secondary button (“Join community”), a “Live” badge, a search field for messages, and photo-led cards for events and sermons. Every final screen is built from these.

Designing the states, not just the look

A component is not finished when it looks right. It is finished when every state it can be in is designed. For the giving flow that meant:

  • The amount field empty, filled and invalid.
  • The giving type (tithe, offering, building project) selected and unselected.
  • The “Give now” button enabled, disabled and loading.
  • A clear confirmation screen, and the giving history one tap away.

Research had told us that giving had to feel safe and quick, and that trust mattered more than extra features. Designing these states up front is how that insight turned into something concrete: two steps from amount to confirmation, with no moment where the person wonders whether it worked.

Where it paid off

WITHOUT A SYSTEMEach journey designed on its own, with small differences everywhere.
WITH THE SYSTEMEvents, communities, media, giving and account all look like one app.
WITHOUT A SYSTEMWireframe feedback means redrawing many screens.
WITH THE SYSTEMWhen testers struggled to find giving, moving it to the tab bar was one change to one component.
WITHOUT A SYSTEMDevelopers measure every screen and ask which value is correct.
WITH THE SYSTEMDevelopers build each component once and reuse it.

The results backed it up. In usability testing, people completed every task, and every test of online giving went through successfully. Members began onboarding onto the app in the first two weeks. A system doesn’t create those results by itself, but it removes a whole category of small confusions that get in the way of them.

What I’d keep doing

  1. Build the system after the wireframes, not before.Grayscale wireframes settle the structure. The system should serve that structure, not decide it.
  2. Keep it small.Four colours, four type sizes and a short component list were enough. Every extra token is one more thing to keep consistent.
  3. Check accessibility at the token level.If every colour pair passes AA in the system, every screen built from it starts in a good place.
  4. Design slots for content that changes.Announcements and sermons change every week, so I designed cards that staff can update without breaking the layout.

THE SHORT VERSION

Decide colours, type, spacing and components once. Design every state they can be in. Then build the screens. It feels slower on day one and saves time on every day after.
