UX Research on a Small Budget

There was no research budget for the Dominion City Kubwa app. There didn’t need to be. Here is what I did instead, and what it told us.

Chibueze Wisdom UgochukwuUI/UX & Product Designer
4 MIN READ

ON THIS PAGE

  1. Start with the people you can reach
  2. Four cheap methods
  3. From notes to insights

When people hear “user research”, they picture recruiting agencies, labs and paid participants. Most small teams, churches and early products have none of that. What they do have is access to the people who use the product, and that is the part that matters.

On the Dominion City Kubwa app, research cost almost nothing but time. It still shaped every main screen.

Start with the people you can already reach

A church has something many startups would envy: its users are in one building every week. Instead of recruiting strangers, I spoke to the people who were already there, and I made sure to hear from both sides:

  • Regular members, who know how church life works and want it to be faster.
  • Recent visitors, who don’t know how it works yet and are the easiest to lose.
  • Staff from the media, admin and follow-up teams, who run everything members see.

Talking to staff is easy to skip, and it is a mistake to skip it. They knew where announcements actually came from, who handled new visitors, and what they would realistically be able to keep updated.

You don’t need a big sample. You need the right mix of people, and to really listen to them.

Four cheap methods that did the heavy lifting

  1. Short interviews.Conversations with members and recent visitors about their week, not just about Sunday. Where do you hear about events? How did you find a group? How do you give?
  2. A simple survey.A free online form about habits, channels and needs, to check whether what I heard in interviews was common or just one person’s view.
  3. A review of other church apps.Free to do, and the fastest way to learn the common patterns for media, giving and events, and where they fall short.
  4. Staff conversations.Quick chats with the teams who would run the app, to make sure every design could actually be kept up to date.

From notes to insights to design decisions

Research is only useful if it changes what you build. I grouped what I heard into four insights, and gave each one a design response:

WHAT WE LEARNEDSunday isn’t the only touchpoint. Members want to know what’s on during the week.
WHAT I DESIGNEDAn events-first home screen showing this week’s gatherings.
WHAT WE LEARNEDFirst-time visitors felt unsure how to connect after their first visit.
WHAT I DESIGNEDOnboarding that ends with a clear “Find a community” step.
WHAT WE LEARNEDPeople rewatch messages to take notes and share them.
WHAT I DESIGNEDA searchable video library with speaker and date.
WHAT WE LEARNEDFor giving, trust and speed mattered more than extra features.
WHAT I DESIGNEDTwo-step giving with a clear confirmation and history.

Notice that none of these needed expensive research to discover. They needed someone to ask, and then to take the answers seriously.

One persona, used every day

I turned the research into a single persona: Tobi, 26, a young professional who is new to the church. His line summed up the problem better than any chart could:

“I enjoyed the service, but I didn’t know how to get more involved.”Tobi, the persona built from member and visitor interviews

Tobi wanted to find events and groups that fit his week, rewatch messages on his commute, and give without hunting for account details. His frustrations were missing announcements made during service, not knowing who to ask about joining a unit, and copying bank details for every offering.

A persona is cheap to make and only valuable if you use it. Every time a design decision was unclear, the question was simple: would this help Tobi?

Test where people actually are

The most useful testing I did didn’t happen in a meeting room. It happened after service, with members who had five minutes and a phone in their hand. Short sessions like that caught problems no design review would have.

The clearest example came from the grayscale wireframes. Testers struggled to find giving when it sat in a menu, so it moved to the tab bar. That one change came from a few minutes of watching real people, and it cost nothing.

THE RESULTS

In usability testing, people completed every task, and every test of online giving went through successfully. Members began onboarding onto the app in its first two weeks.

If you only remember three things

  • Talk to the people you can already reach, and include the people who run the product, not only the people who use it.
  • Turn every insight into a specific design response, or it was just a nice conversation.
  • Test in the real setting, even for five minutes at a time.
